The Paper Lantern and the Forest Beetle

Last Sunday morning, my classmate Sora and I were sitting silently on wooden mats inside an old cedar hall at the Green Valley Nature Center, trying to count our breaths for forty minutes. I had signed up for this "Digital Detox & Forest Mindfulness" day camp to learn how to calm my racing thoughts before our winter university entrance mock exams. To be honest, it wasn't my own idea at first; my older sister, who had attended the same workshop two years ago when she was struggling with exam stress, had strongly recommended it to me over dinner last month. She told me that leaving my smartphone in a locker for a single Sunday might help me reset my concentration. Sora decided to come along too because he had been losing sleep over our basketball team's upcoming tournament.

Before the morning meditation session started at 10:00 AM, all twenty participants had gathered at the log cabin reception desk at 9:15 AM to hand in their smartphones and receive a handmade paper lantern for the afternoon forest walk. While we were waiting in line at the reception desk, Sora accidentally bumped his elbow against a wooden shelf, knocking over a display basket of pine cones. We hurriedly picked them up together, laughing nervously. Then, we entered the quiet cedar hall and sat down in two neat rows facing the open garden windows.

About twenty minutes into the silent breathing session, I heard a low, rhythmic scratching sound near my right knee. Little by little, the sound grew louder. Opening one eye slightly, I saw a large, shiny brown stag beetle climbing up the sleeve of my sweater! I was startled and instinctively raised my left hand to flick it away, but our instructor, Ms. Wakabayashi, had told us at the beginning not to make sudden movements or loud noises during the session. My heart started beating fast, and I wondered what on earth I should do.

Just then, Ms. Wakabayashi noticed my frozen posture. Moving without a sound, she picked up a fallen magnolia leaf and a small bamboo tea saucer from a side table. She knelt beside me and gently held the magnolia leaf in front of the beetle's legs. As soon as the beetle crawled onto the leaf, she covered it loosely with the bamboo saucer so it wouldn't fly into anyone's hair. Instead of throwing the insect roughly outside, she walked slowly to the wooden veranda, knelt down, and placed the leaf on the mossy root of an oak tree. Watching the beetle walk safely into the moss, she smiled warmly at me.

After the session ended at noon, Ms. Wakabayashi came over to me while we were drinking warm roasted green tea. "When an unexpected trouble crawls onto your sleeve—whether it's a forest beetle or a difficult exam question—fighting it in panic only breaks your peace," she said gently. "Acknowledge it calmly, guide it with patience, and let it pass." In the afternoon, Sora and I walked the three-kilometer forest trail carrying our paper lanterns and took a commemorative polaroid photo with Ms. Wakabayashi in front of the oak tree before heading home at 4:00 PM. I don't know yet whether my next mock exam score will jump immediately, but that tiny beetle taught me the true meaning of a steady mind.

What Makes an Ideal Learning & Club Space?

By Dr. Diana Bashworth, Editor at Campus Design Quarterly

As schools and universities work to improve their study lounges and club rooms, it is helpful to follow a clear design framework. The SIN Model—which stands for Stimulation, Individualization, and Naturalness—provides practical guidelines for creating productive spaces.

First, Stimulation relates to visual color and complexity across walls, floors, and displays. A room with bare gray walls feels dull, yet a room with too many bright posters and cluttered shelves is visually distracting. Designers recommend painting one accent wall a calm, inviting color and keeping 20% to 30% of wall space completely free of posters or shelves.

Second, Individualization includes two elements: ownership and flexibility. Ownership means students feel the room belongs to them—for example, by having personal lockers and a neat display shelf for club trophies or current projects. Flexibility means using lightweight, wheeled desks and stackable chairs so the room can be quickly rearranged for quiet individual reading, small-group chatting, or large debate practice.

Third, Naturalness concerns light, temperature, and air quality. Too much direct sunlight through bare windows creates blinding glare on projector screens and whiteboards, while the opposite corner of the room may remain too dark for reading. Furthermore, poorly ventilated rooms become hot and stuffy in summer. Installing adjustable window blinds, balanced LED ceiling lights, and a quiet air-circulation fan is essential.

Rhythms of Rest: How Brains Recharge Day and Night

Why do all animals with a nervous system need to rest, even when resting leaves them vulnerable to predators? Modern neurobiology reveals that sleep is far from a passive shutdown. During sleep, the brain repairs cellular damage, consolidates newly learned skills into long-term memory, and flushes out metabolic waste proteins that accumulate between neurons during waking hours. Contrary to an old myth, sleep is not meant to permanently raise an animal's core body temperature; in fact, body temperature naturally drops slightly during deep rest to conserve energy.

Across the animal kingdom, species have evolved remarkable ways to balance rest with survival. For example, great frigatebirds—seabirds that fly non-stop over the Pacific Ocean for weeks—utilize "unihemispheric slow-wave sleep." While gliding on rising air currents, they rest one half of their brain at a time while keeping the other half awake and one eye open to watch their flight path. Meanwhile, some organisms without a central brain, such as box jellyfish, enter a sleep-like relaxation state on the sea floor at night, and black bears enter winter hibernation—a seasonal state of torpor that saves energy when food is scarce, though it differs neurologically from daily sleep.

What about humans during the daytime? Chronobiologists have discovered that the 24-hour circadian clock is not the only rhythm governing our minds. Throughout the day, human alertness rises and falls in 90-to-120-minute waves called "ultradian rhythms." After roughly 90 minutes of intense mental focus, the brain's prefrontal cortex begins to run low on glucose and neurotransmitters, causing concentration to dip and mistakes to rise.

To work with—rather than against—these biological rhythms, sleep scientists recommend two practical habits. First, after every 90 minutes of focused study, step away from digital screens for 15 minutes to let your eyes view natural daylight or greenery, or do light physical movement such as stretching or walking outdoors. Checking social media on a smartphone during a break does not rest the visual cortex! Second, to protect nighttime sleep cycles, avoid caffeinated drinks like strong green tea or coffee late at night, and avoid staring at bright phone screens immediately after waking up or right before sleeping.
